01

Venture Capital Investors

Venture Capital (VC) is a form of investment in young companies that investors believe have the potential to grow rapidly.

A startup may have a strong idea, technology or business model but may not have enough money to develop its product, hire employees, enter new markets or expand internationally.

A venture capital investor provides capital to the startup, normally in return for an ownership interest in the company.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

Imagine that three graduates create an artificial-intelligence startup.

Their company needs US$2 million to develop its technology and expand.

A venture capital fund invests US$2 million in the startup in exchange for an ownership stake.

If the startup becomes extremely successful, the investment may become worth much more. If the company fails, the investor may lose much or all of the investment.

What do VC investors look for?

  • The founders and their experience
  • The business idea
  • The technology or product
  • The size of the potential market
  • Revenue and growth potential
  • Competitive advantage
  • Scalability of the business
  • Previous funding and investors
  • Ability of the management team

VC investment is not the same as a bank loan

Suppose a startup borrows ₹5 crore from a bank.

The company normally has to repay the loan, together with interest.

A VC investor normally invests in exchange for an ownership interest and therefore takes the risk that the company may fail.

SIMPLE COMPARISON

Bank: "I lend you money. You repay me."

VC investor: "I invest in your company and receive an ownership interest. If the company becomes very successful, my investment may become much more valuable."

Why is Venture Capital important for PitchBook?

PitchBook's university rankings are connected to founders whose companies have received venture funding.

Therefore, PitchBook is not simply asking:

Which universities have produced the most businessmen?

It is looking at a much more specific entrepreneurship and venture-backed company universe.

02

How Does a Founder Qualify for the PitchBook University Rankings?

PitchBook does not simply count every person who starts a business. For its university founder rankings, PitchBook examines founders whose companies received a qualifying venture-funding round during the ranking's measurement period.

For the 2026 PitchBook University Rankings, the qualifying funding period is:

January 1, 2015 → August 1, 2026

Therefore, a founder may qualify when his or her company received at least one qualifying round of funding during this period. The amount raised does not have to be the same for every company. What matters is whether the financing falls within one of the qualifying venture-funding categories used in the ranking.

1. Seed Funding

Seed funding is normally one of the earliest formal stages of financing for a startup.

The money may be used to develop the first product, test the market, recruit an initial team or establish whether the business idea can become commercially viable.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

An IIT Bombay graduate creates an artificial-intelligence startup in 2023.

The company is still developing its first commercial product and raises US$1.5 million from investors in a seed round.

Because Seed is one of the qualifying funding categories, the founder can potentially be included in PitchBook's university founder analysis.

2. Early Stage VC

Early Stage Venture Capital normally applies after the company has progressed beyond the earliest idea stage and is developing a viable product, customers or commercial operations.

PitchBook generally associates early-stage venture financing with rounds such as Series A and Series B, although its classification can also consider the company's age, previous financing, company status and participating investors.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

An IIT Delhi alumnus establishes a software company.

After proving that customers are willing to use the product, the company raises a US$8 million Series A round from a venture capital fund.

This would ordinarily fall within the Early Stage VC category and can make the founder relevant to the PitchBook university ranking methodology.

3. Later Stage VC

Later Stage Venture Capital generally applies to more developed venture-backed companies that have already completed earlier funding rounds and are raising additional capital for expansion.

PitchBook generally associates later-stage VC with Series C, Series D and other later rounds, subject to its deal-classification methodology.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

An ISB alumnus creates a financial-technology company.

The startup previously completed Seed, Series A and Series B rounds.

It now operates in several countries and raises US$60 million in Series C funding.

This represents Later Stage VC funding and the founder can potentially be counted in the relevant university founder statistics.

4. Angel Funding

Angel funding is generally an investment made by an individual investor using personal capital rather than money managed through a conventional venture capital fund.

Angel investors are especially common during the earliest stages of a startup.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A University of Mumbai graduate creates an online education company.

A successful entrepreneur personally invests ₹40 lakh in the company in exchange for equity.

If PitchBook classifies the transaction as an Angel financing round, it falls within a qualifying category used for the university rankings.

5. Restart Early Stage VC

A Restart financing category is used when a company's financing history has effectively been reset or the company is beginning a new venture-financing cycle after significant changes to its previous financing situation.

Restart Early Stage VC refers to such a restarted company receiving financing that PitchBook classifies at the early-stage venture level.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A technology company previously raised investment but later underwent a major restructuring.

New investors enter, the ownership structure is substantially reorganised and the company begins a new growth phase.

It then raises US$6 million in a financing classified by PitchBook as Restart Early Stage VC.

This category is included among the qualifying financing types for the university founder rankings.

6. Restart Later Stage VC

Restart Later Stage VC involves a company whose venture financing history has been restarted but which is being financed at a more mature stage of development.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A mature startup encounters major financial difficulties and subsequently restructures its capitalization.

A new investor group provides US$35 million to recapitalize and expand the business.

If PitchBook categorizes this transaction as Restart Later Stage VC, the financing can qualify for the university ranking methodology.

7. Restart Angel

Restart Angel refers to an angel financing associated with a company whose financing cycle has effectively restarted.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A startup had previously raised financing but later changed ownership, strategy or capitalization substantially.

During its restarted phase, an individual investor personally invests ₹75 lakh.

If PitchBook records the deal as Restart Angel, the financing belongs to one of the categories included in the founder-ranking methodology.

Does the Founder Have to Raise a Minimum Amount?

The university-ranking methodology does not publish one universal minimum requirement such as US$1 million, US$5 million or ₹10 crore that every founder must raise.

The important question is whether the founder's company received a financing round that PitchBook recognizes within one of the qualifying categories during the applicable ranking period.

What if the Founder Studied at More Than One University?

IIT BOMBAY → WORK EXPERIENCE → ISB → STARTUP

Suppose a student completes engineering at IIT Bombay.

The person then works for five years and subsequently completes an MBA or management programme at ISB.

After ISB, the person establishes a startup and the company receives qualifying venture funding.

Because PitchBook allows a founder's educational history to include more than one institution, the same founder can potentially contribute to the statistics of both IIT Bombay and ISB, subject to the applicable undergraduate, graduate or MBA ranking category.

HOW MANY YEARS OF FUNDING DATA DOES PITCHBOOK CONSIDER?

For the 2026 University Rankings, PitchBook counts qualifying founders whose companies received venture funding between January 1, 2015 and August 1, 2026.

PitchBook describes the ranking as examining roughly the previous decade of venture-backed founder activity. The exact published 2026 date range covers approximately 11 years and 7 months.

This measurement period moves forward when PitchBook publishes a new annual university ranking. Therefore, Founder Matrix should display the specific ranking year and funding window instead of assuming that every year's ranking uses exactly the same fixed dates.

02

Angel Investors

The correct term is Angel Investor, not "Angle Investor".

An angel investor is generally an individual who invests his or her own money in an early-stage company.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

Three students start a technology company.

They need ₹50 lakh to build their first product.

A successful entrepreneur personally invests ₹20 lakh in the startup.

That individual is acting as an angel investor.

Angel Investor vs Venture Capital Investor

An angel investor is generally an individual investing personal money.

A venture capital investor is generally a professional investment fund or VC firm investing capital raised for investment.

ANOTHER EXAMPLE

A founder starts a company and receives ₹25 lakh from a wealthy entrepreneur who invests personal money.

Later, when the company requires ₹10 crore, a venture capital fund invests in the business.

The startup has therefore moved from early individual investment towards institutional venture capital.

What can an angel investor provide?

  • Early-stage capital
  • Business experience
  • Mentoring
  • Industry contacts
  • Customer introductions
  • Hiring connections
  • Business credibility

Angel investment and venture capital are both important forms of startup financing, but they are not identical.

Indian Universities & Institutions Featured in Founder Matrix

The following Indian institutions are featured because they are highly relevant to discussions about technology, management, business, entrepreneurship, alumni networks and venture-backed founders.

Their inclusion does not mean that they are the only good institutions in India.

It also does not mean that PitchBook has declared these eight institutions to be the eight best universities in India overall.

PitchBook measures a specific dimension: representation of founders and their venture-backed companies.

3.1 Indian School of Business (ISB)

Established: 2001

Location: Hyderabad, Telangana and Mohali, Punjab

ISB is primarily a graduate business school. It is therefore different from a broad undergraduate university such as the University of Mumbai or University of Delhi.

Courses and programmes

ISB offers management and executive programmes including:

  • PGP in Management
  • PGP for Young Leaders
  • PGP MFAB for family businesses
  • PGP PRO for working professionals
  • PGP MAX for senior executives
  • Executive education programmes
  • Doctoral-level research programmes
Fees

ISB is a premium management education institution and its flagship programmes involve substantial fees.

Fees vary according to programme, academic year, accommodation and other components. Students should therefore check the current official fee structure before making a financial decision.

Faculty

ISB's faculty covers areas such as:

  • Finance
  • Marketing
  • Strategy
  • Operations
  • Economics
  • Entrepreneurship
  • Organisational behaviour
  • Information systems
  • Business analytics
  • Public policy
Salary and career outcomes

ISB students are generally experienced professionals rather than conventional fresh undergraduate students.

Therefore, ISB salary statistics should be understood as career outcomes after advanced management education rather than as the starting salary of a typical undergraduate.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A software engineer works for several years and then joins ISB.

After ISB, the person moves into consulting, product management, investment banking or entrepreneurship.

If the person subsequently becomes a founder of a VC-backed company, that educational history becomes relevant to the PitchBook Founder Matrix.

Why is ISB featured?

ISB is particularly relevant because it combines management education, experienced professionals, business networks and entrepreneurship.

Its alumni may already have substantial professional experience before becoming founders.

Therefore, ISB is particularly relevant to understanding the relationship between graduate management education and entrepreneurship.

How to Read PitchBook Founder Rankings

PitchBook should not be read in the same way as a conventional academic ranking such as QS, THE or ARWU.

PitchBook focuses on a different dimension: the relationship between universities and founders associated with venture-backed companies.

4.1 Undergraduates

Undergraduates refers to founders whose educational history includes undergraduate study at the institution.

It does not mean that the person necessarily founded the company while studying for the undergraduate degree.

EXAMPLE

Rahul studies Computer Science at IIT Bombay from 2012 to 2016.

He works in the technology industry for several years.

In 2022, he creates an AI startup.

The startup later receives venture capital.

His IIT Bombay undergraduate education can therefore be relevant to the PitchBook undergraduate founder category.

4.2 Graduates

The Graduate category concerns founders whose relevant educational history includes graduate-level study.

EXAMPLE

A student completes a bachelor's degree at University A.

The student later completes a master's degree at University B.

Years later, the person becomes the founder of a VC-backed company.

The graduate education at University B can therefore be relevant to the graduate-level founder analysis.

4.3 MBA

MBA is a separate category specifically related to founders associated with Master of Business Administration education.

EXAMPLE

A person completes engineering at IIT Delhi.

The person later completes an MBA at IIM Bangalore.

Several years later, the person starts a technology company that receives venture funding.

The founder's IIM Bangalore MBA education can therefore be relevant to the PitchBook MBA category.

4.4 Founder Count

Founder Count represents the number of relevant founders represented in the PitchBook dataset for that institution and category.

EXAMPLE

Suppose a university has:

Founder Count = 840

This does not mean that 840 founders are currently on the university campus.

It means that 840 relevant founders are represented in the applicable PitchBook founder dataset.

4.5 Company Count

Company Count represents the number of relevant companies associated with the founders in the dataset.

EXAMPLE

Imagine five graduates from the same university create one startup together.

There could therefore be:

5 founders

but only:

1 company

This explains why Founder Count and Company Count are not necessarily equal.

4.6 Capital Raised

Capital Raised refers to funding raised by the relevant companies represented in the PitchBook dataset.

It is not the university's income.

It is not the university's budget.

It is not the founder's personal wealth.

It is not company revenue.

It is not necessarily company valuation.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

Suppose three relevant companies raised:

Company A: US$100 million

Company B: US$400 million

Company C: US$500 million

Combined capital raised: US$1 billion

The Founder Matrix would therefore display approximately US$1 billion for that combined dataset.

4.7 Read All the Numbers Together

Do not look only at the PitchBook rank. Look at:

  • Rank
  • Founder Count
  • Company Count
  • Capital Raised
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

Suppose University A is ranked: #10

Founder Count: 500

Company Count: 350

Capital Raised: US$20 billion

This tells you much more than simply saying "University A is ranked #10."

4.8 Why Can a University Have Many Founders but Less Capital?

Because startups are different in size and funding requirements.

EXAMPLE

University A has:

500 founders

but their companies collectively raised:

US$1 billion

University B has:

200 founders

but several of those founders created very large companies.

Their companies collectively raised:

US$10 billion

Therefore, more founders does not automatically mean more capital raised.

4.9 PitchBook is NOT a Conventional Academic Ranking

A conventional university ranking may focus heavily on areas such as:

  • Academic reputation
  • Research
  • Citations
  • Internationalization
  • Employer reputation

PitchBook focuses on a very different dimension:

Universities → Founders → Companies → Venture Capital → Capital Raised

Therefore, a university ranked highly by PitchBook is not automatically academically superior to every university ranked lower.

It means that the institution has strong representation within the specific venture-backed founder ecosystem measured by PitchBook.

THE FOUNDER MATRIX IN ONE SENTENCE

PitchBook Founder Matrix measures the connection between universities and the entrepreneurs, companies and venture capital associated with their alumni — not the overall academic quality of the university.
